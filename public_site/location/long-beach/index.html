Long Beach historic-property and earthquake evidence plan

Separate architectural significance from movable-property provenance and record vulnerable objects before shaking or relocation changes them

Long Beach’s Historic Preservation program documents the city’s approach to historic resources, while the Disaster Preparedness bureau covers local hazards and readiness. A historic building may direct research toward permits, archives, earlier owners, or period photographs, but its status does not authenticate an artwork, furnishing, document, or collectible found there. Earthquake and emergency planning add a second need: a dated record of the item, its installation, and its existing condition before movement, detachment, impact, or emergency handling alters the evidence.

Prove the link one transfer at a time

State the claimed connection among object, address, room, owner, and date. Search for object-specific support such as bills of sale, inventories, correspondence, exhibition records, restoration files, inscriptions, labels, and dated interiors. Photograph the object in place, its scale relative to the room, every side, marks, back or underside, joinery, mount, frame, wall anchors, base, and any alteration made to fit the space. Treat the property record, oral history, and evaluator’s conclusion as separate evidence layers.

Record installation and post-event changes safely

Before an inspection, ask about wall anchoring, shelving, glass, overhead hazards, heavy furniture, elevator status, utilities, parking, stairs, and exits. Do not remove unstable glass, lift fallen stone, reconnect electrical objects, or improvise repairs for photographs. After an event, preserve a wide view before moving detached elements and label every fragment to its parent item. Use the preparation checklist for an off-site review, and follow official access or evacuation directions before scheduling direct examination.
